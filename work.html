About ROAR

Established in 2019, the UC Berkeley Robot Open Autonomous Racing (ROAR) program aims to advance solutions of autonomous systems, intelligent machines, and human-in-the-loop control for extreme robotics applications.
To accomplish this, in part, ROAR provides resources for high school students to explore these fields.
I am not a member of the UC Berkeley ROAR team. Statements made here should not be made to construe any relationship of this manner.

From 2022 to 2026, I have worked extensively with the ROAR platform and competition environment.
In 2025, I was awarded $2,000 of funding by Berkeley to explore autonomous driving and host outreach programs during my ambassadorship term.

An [extended] example of my thinking processes
The core of the testing vehicle (pictured below) 's navigation module was built around Giseop Kim's PyICP-SLAM repository, which was modified to read live LiDAR scans from the YDLidar G4 unit their SDK's Python API. PyICP-SLAM's odometry data output could immediately be extended to provide mapping capabilities, used to align all scans with the current pose.

The G4 produces around ~400 to 600 valid points per scan. In controlled testing (around 20 scans taken manually at varying and/or even intervals), this point density could achieve centimeter to millimeter level odometry accuracy even when breaking PyICP-SLAM's constant velocity assumption.

However, upon live testing, it was discovered that the pose estimation was subject to rapid and significant accumulating error while the vehicle remained stationary. The loop closure function could successfully account for this error by "snapping back" the vehicle to origin, but stalls the control code for a large period of time in the process of doing so. Combining two scans together mostly alleviated this with the relatively arbitrary parameters of icp_tries=50 and icp_tolerance=0.00000001, but reduces the effective maximum "scan rate" of the LiDAR from 12Hz to 6Hz and increases the processing time needed to calculate odometry data and mapping.

In order to convert raw discrete lidar data into a form that can be used for path planning, I looked to the intuition that traditional cartesian coordinates describe the same thing as a spreadsheet's rows and columns ("battleship coordinates"), only with infinite precision and thereby "resolution." By truncating trailing decimals from scan data, large point counts could be culled by simplifying the working representation of the environment as a grid, wherein the more decimals truncated corresponds to a greater cell size and decreased resolution.

This helps "seal gaps" in between points and construct continuous barriers, while the representation of the environment as cells in a grid is already conducive to the usage of pathfinding algorithms like Dijkstra and A* by virtue of looking like a Graph and quacking like a Graph. To help the algorithm avoid pathing close to obstacles and to further seal gaps, an "infection" or "marching" process is further applied to each new cell that recursively assigns decreasing weights/costs to neighbors to discourage A*/Dijkstra from hugging walls while still allowing it to path through small gaps.

The nature of the LiDAR scans posed an additional challenge in that cell counts could easily enter the millions if maps were saved and instantiated in full. While I did not perform a formal evaluation of performance differences between appreciably large arrays and the current approach, I opted instead to only save occupied points and allow A* to generate and add new points to a dictionary on the fly as they are "discovered" by the algorithm. With a cell resolution of 10cm by 10cm, A* completes nearly instantaneously, with mapbuilding and ICP taking up most of the computation time per loop.

Given the path generated by A*, it is relatively easy to obtain a set of waypoints that can be fed into the familiar ROAR competition format. Code originally used to interface with the CARLA simulation and ROAR waypoints format was cloned and adapted to work with the given hardware instead.
